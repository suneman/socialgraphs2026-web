The new way of working

This course was rebuilt in 2026, from the ground up, around one fact: you have AI tools that can do most of what used to be homework. Here is what we changed, why we changed it, and what it asks of you. It'll take ten minutes of your time and should explain every rule you will meet all semester.

The same argument, to camera.

1 · What changed

In 2025 this course graded you on two take-home assignments and a project. Around mid way, I sat down with the best agentic coding tools and tried to see how well it would do on the assignments. For someone trying to build a great course for students, it was a bit disheartening. Most of the pipeline — scrape the data, compute the statistics, find the communities, run the sentiment analysis, write it up — could be one-shot by a machine. Not "helped along". Solved.

So I felt there were two things I could do. Pretend that nothing happened, maybe police harder: ban the tools, run detectors, pretend it is still 2019.

Or try something exciting. Redesign! Accept that these tools are now part of how science gets done — they are in the labs, they will be in your jobs, and rebuild the sh#t out of the course so that using agentic coding tools well is part of the skill being taught. I love me a challenge (actually I find it pretty exciting), so of course I chose the second option.

2 · The split

We use paper to ensure understanding.
We use agents to explore the world.

The whole redesign is that one split, applied consistently.

We need to change your brain, you need to know stuff. Reading a degree distribution. Knowing what a null model is for. Predicting what happens to a network when a parameter moves. Having the intuition to identify a broken analysis. These are not typing skills, and no tool supplies them when you need them: in a meeting, refereeing a claim, deciding whether your own machine's output makes sense. So twice in the semester you must sit in a room with a pen and paper, closed book, and show us the concepts are actually in there. That is what the two midterms are, and they are the only place the course restricts your tools.

Everything else assumes the machine. The weekly exercises, the go-nuts posts, the project: use every tool you have, and expect us to raise the bar accordingly. Each group's project crawls its own live Wikipedia domain. Deliverables are interactive, public, real. The finished projects should look like things no student could have shipped in a semester five years ago. Because with your new tools, you can.

3 · The three modes

Every exercise in this course carries exactly one label, so you always know which game you are playing:

  • 🧠 Learn — AI hands-off. This is midterm material: concepts, hand-calculations, interpretation. Doing these with a machine is not cheating so much as pointless. Like driving to the gym to have a robot lift the weights. The midterm is where you find out whether you did them.
  • 🔬 Tool — the LLM is a tool. You use a model to measure something: label sentiment, extract entities, classify pages. A measurement tool you haven't calibrated is a random number generator with good manners. So in this mode the deliverable is never just the measurement, it is the validation: ground-truth samples you labeled yourself, agreement statistics, an error analysis.
  • 🚀 Builder — full agentic mode. Point your tools at an ambitious goal and go. You are judged on the outcome and on your ability to defend every choice in it. Because when you demo, you stand next to your work and take questions, live. So you'll need to know how that community detection actually works.

4 · The skill underneath

Notice what the 🔬 mode is really teaching. The new scientific skill of your generation is not prompting. It is knowing when to trust the machine. An LLM that labels your data is a tool, like a thermometer or a survey: sometimes accurate, sometimes biased, always confident. Scientists have a hundred years of habits for exactly this situation — sample, compare against ground truth, quantify agreement, report the error. We will make you practice those habits until they are reflexes, and your project repo will contain an AI_METHODS.md documenting every place a model touched your results and how you checked it. At the demo fair, "the model said so" is not a method.

5 · The deal

So this is the arrangement, stated plainly. We will never grade you on work a machine can do. In exchange: you keep the machine out of the material that has to live in your head, you calibrate it wherever it touches your data, and you own everything you submit. Hold up your end and you leave this course with the two things worth having: a head that understands networks without assistance, and a portfolio of work at a level that assistance made possible.

6 · The practical elements

Everything above is philosophy. Here is the machinery, in one place — this section is linkable (#practical) and every rule below holds all semester.

  • When and where. Wednesdays from 09:00, Auditorium 43, building 303A (auditorium 44 and classroom 46 are ours too when we need the space).
  • How the day runs. There are no lectures to sit through — the content lives on this site, in the readings and the videos. At 09:00 there is a short pep talk by moi framing the week's ideas; after that you work through the week's page with your group at your own pace. Readings, explorables, exercises in your own notebooks, with the TAs and me circulating all day to help. Leave when the week's work is done (or catch up before next time).
  • Groups of three. You work in a 3-person group, formed in week 1 and kept all semester (within reason): weekly exercises, the go-nuts site, both project stages, etc. One iron rule: everyone must be able to solve everything — the midterms are individual, on paper, and they do not care who in your group understood it.
  • The two rooms that matter twice. The midterms (30 September and 4 November) run in-class, on paper, across all three rooms; room assignments are published in advance. The demo fair (2 December) is live, in person, and the whole group stands next to its work.
