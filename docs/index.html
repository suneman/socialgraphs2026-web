How this course works

▶ The course starts here

Before anything else, read The new way of working. It's going to take ten minutes, but I hope you'll find it worth your while. It explains why this course runs the way it does, what the components of the course are ... and a couple of practical things. Some of it is below in a short version.

You have powerful AI tools. We know. This course is built to embrace that fact rather than fight it: we will never grade you on typing code an agent can type for you. Instead, the course asks for two different things, kept strictly apart:

  • Concepts in your head. Twice during the semester you sit an in-class, pen-and-paper test. Closed book, no devices. If you can't read a degree distribution or spot a broken null model without a machine, you don't understand it yet.
  • Ambition in your work. Everywhere else, use every tool you have. The bar moves up accordingly: we'll be using real datasets you crawl yourself, interactive visualizations, simulations, complex tools. Things that there's no way we could have even imagined doing in 2020.

And there is a brand new scientific skill that I'll try to push you to use all semester: validating what the machine tells you. An LLM that labels your data is a tool, and tools need to be calibrated, tested and checked.

Every exercise carries one of three modes

🧠 Learn

AI hands-off. This is the material the tests draw from — concepts, calculations, interpretation. Do it with your head, your group, and paper.

🔬 Tool

LLMs are our tools. They annotate text, embed documents, etc. You must validate the output the way a scientist validates any tool: ground-truth samples, agreement statistics.

🚀 Builder

Full agentic mode. Judged on the outcome, the rigor behind it, and whether you can explain and defend every choice. Ideally defend it live and in person.

The weeks

Flipped classroom: before class you catch up on last week; in class you work the exercises in your 3-person group. Weeks 1–4 are the networks half, weeks 5–7 the language half, week 8 is where they meet. Every week ends with a Go nuts with your LLM exercise. It's a free-form, agent-powered analysis on a shared dataset, published on your group's own site, with a weekly winner. Weeks 9–13 are the project period.

Midterms are ~60 minutes, ~25 multiple-choice questions, closed book. They test transfer, not recall: reading figures, predicting outcomes, spotting flawed analyses (including AI-generated ones), small hand calculations, and choosing the right method. Work is scored on a 0–3 scale per component and mapped holistically to the final grade.

AI policy, in one paragraph

Outside the two midterms, AI use is not just allowed. It's assumed, encouraged, and often required. Two rules are absolute. One: respect the mode label on every exercise; 🧠 Learn-mode work is done without AI because it's the material you'll face on paper (and you need it inside your human brain to "drive" the LLMs the right way). Two: you are responsible for everything you submit — every AI-derived measurement must be validated and documented (your project repo must include an AI_METHODS.md). "The model said so" is not a method.

The final project

In your group, pick a domain, crawl it, and find something true that nobody knew — a real research finding combining network structure and text. Four deliverables:

DeliverableWhat it is
Pitch video1 minute: the research idea, the data, why it matters. Feedback session the week after.
Data storyAn interactive public web page that walks a reader through your finding — the artifact you'll show people for years.
Short paper3 pages, PNAS format: abstract, intro, results, methods. Science still gets written down.
Demo fairFinal session, poster-style: live demos, ~10 minutes of questions per group. You explain your own work.

The book

Michele Coscia, The Atlas for the Aspiring Network Scientist (2nd edition) — free PDF, with per-chapter exercises and solutions on the site. Weekly pages point to exact chapters. A few classic papers join it along the way.
